Family House Rules

Standard rules are for strangers. Every family that plays year over year builds a private body of law — and every law is named after the relative whose behavior required it. Welcome to the Aunt Karen Addendum.

Legally binding · orally transmitted

Quick answer

Family house rules are the private body of law that grows around a white elephant game that is played year after year: something happens, a rule follows, and eventually the rule is read aloud before the game. Naming each rule after the relative who caused it turns a grudge into a tradition.

How white elephant law actually works

The standard rulebook — numbers, pick-or-steal, the Three-Owner Rule, Player 1's final swap — is the constitution. But families don't play by constitutions; they play by precedent. Year one, something happens. Year two, a rule exists because of it. Year ten, the rule is read aloud before the game like a court convening, and nobody remembers that the rule was ever not there. The magic is in the naming: a rule named after its originator converts a grudge into a tradition. You don't stay angry at Aunt Karen — you cite her, annually, forever.

The classic family statutes

The Aunt Karen Addendum

Enacted: the year after the Incident

The canonical case. One year, Aunt Karen didn't just steal — she coordinated. Working with other members of her family, she pre-arranged a steal-and-lock scheme: her allies would steal her target gift on schedule, each transfer counted toward the lock, and by the time anyone understood the geometry, the Bailey's was frozen in her hands and three relatives were in on it. The room was furious. The room was also, privately, impressed.

Enacted clause: Collusion and pre-arranged steal-and-lock deals between players are banned. Any coordinated steal is voided on discovery — and is henceforth referred to, permanently, as "Karen-level coordination." The phrase outlived the rule. So did Aunt Karen's denials.

The Youngest Player Protection Act

Enacted: annually, by demand of the youngest

The oldest law on the books, re-passed every single year without exception: the youngest participant is always at risk to cry when their favorite gift is taken from them. It has happened in every family, in every generation, and it will happen again — the Act exists so that when it does, the machinery of mercy is already running.

Enacted clause: The youngest player's first gift may not be stolen until they've held it for one full round. They are seated between two designated non-stealers. The consolation bowl (candy division) covers all resulting tears. Age-tuned variants apply by birthday-party tier.

The Grandpa's Whiskey Amnesty

Enacted: after Grandpa's third whiskey

Certain relatives play differently after the egg nog. The Amnesty declares that steals executed by designated elders after hour two are governed by "grandfathered passion" — the steal stands, the apology is implied, and any objection must be delivered in the form of a toast.

Enacted clause: Steals by declared amnesty-holders cannot be disputed. Counter-steals, however, are encouraged and historically inevitable.

The Marquee Rule (a.k.a. "Model It")

Enacted: after the Snuggie Incident

The MC's favorite statute: any player who receives a wearable or ridiculous gift may be called upon by the Master of Ceremonies to model it for the room. Born the year Uncle Dave received an enormous fleece snuggie-adjacent garment and was ruled to have "forfeited his privacy by opening it."

Enacted clause: The MC may request one modeling walk per ridiculous gift — and the rule follows the gift: if it is stolen, the thief wears it, walking on the spot in front of the player they just robbed. Declining costs one steal token. Performing it earns the crowd's eternal respect. See the MC playbook for the full comedy toolkit.

The Legend Returns Clause (a.k.a. the Pantry Gambit)

Enacted: the year the Bailey's came back

Favorite gifts may reappear the next year — or two, or five years later. A large gift that won the day in a year past can be redeployed, but never naively: the current holder re-wraps it in a small, unassuming box, and the real gift is hidden elsewhere in the house. During the game, the small box is unwrapped to reveal nothing but a card — "Go into the Pantry for your gift." The room erupts. One player leaves the circle on a quest while everyone else imagines what else the host hid. It creates intrigue, mystery, and a second game of "what ELSE did they hide?" that runs all night.

Enacted clause: Prior winners may be redeployed in any year, hidden anywhere in the home, with a clue card in their place. Treasure-hunt reveals are legal, encouraged, and capped only by the host's real estate. Repeat champions get no sympathy and no exemption. Quotable summary on the FAQ page.

The Lookback Rule (a.k.a. the Résumé Review)

Enacted: after the third duplicate candle year

Before the game begins, the MC reads the room its own history: the last three years' most-stolen gifts and reigning champions are reviewed aloud, so everyone sees what was chosen recently. Nobody accidentally re-brings the blanket that won two years running; would-be schemers learn exactly which legends are still in circulation; and new members get the family's steal history as context. It takes ninety seconds and prevents the most awkward white elephant moment there is: unwrapping your carefully chosen gift while someone mutters "we had that in 2023."

Enacted clause: The MC reviews the last three years' winning and most-stolen gifts before the first turn. Bringers of duplicates are mocked gently and taxed one consolation-bowl candy. Quotable summary on the FAQ page.

The Amendment Doctrine

Standing rule: every year, one amendment

The healthiest families add exactly one new rule per year — no more, no fewer. The annual amendment is proposed during dessert, debated over dishes, voted on informally (loudest table wins), and named after whoever caused it. This is how the rulebook grows: not by design, but by scandal.

Enacted clause: This year's amendment goes in the book. Next year's goes in the history books.

How to legislate your own family's law

Three steps, practiced by families nationwide whether they know it or not. One: when an incident occurs — and one will occur — do not litigate it at the party. Let it marinate for a year; distance converts outrage into material. Two: at the next exchange, announce the new rule and name it after its originator, in front of everyone, with eye contact. Three: read the full rulebook aloud before each game, like a court convening — the FAQ covers the standard disputes — and let the named rules get their applause. Within a decade you will have a document so specific that no other family could possibly adopt it — which is the point. For the mechanics the laws modify, see the standard rulebook.

Every law needs an enforcer

House rules only bind when someone reads them aloud, voids the Karen-level steals, and calls the modeling walks. That's the MC's job description.

House rules FAQ

What is the Aunt Karen Addendum?

The rule banning collusive steal-and-lock schemes, named after the relative who ran one — her family pre-arranged steals to freeze a coveted gift on schedule. Coordinated steals get voided and permanently cited as "Karen-level coordination."

Why name rules after relatives?

It converts a grudge into a tradition: the family laughs about the incident annually instead of relitigating it, and the rule becomes an heirloom with a story attached.

What's the Youngest Player Protection Act?

The standing rule that the youngest participant is always at risk to cry when a favorite gift is taken: their first gift can't be stolen for one full round, they sit between non-stealers, and the consolation bowl handles the tears.

How many house rules should a family have?

One new amendment per year, maximum. A rulebook that grows faster than one statute a year stops being funny and starts being a HOA.

What if the namesake objects to their rule?

They can move to strike at the annual amendment vote — whereupon the rule is renamed in their honor, which is considered a promotion.
