Yankee Swap

The Bay State didn't just rename the white elephant gift exchange — it built a tradition of desirable gifts, quiet scheming, and Civil-War-era storytelling around it.

Swap, don't steal — wink

Quick answer

A Yankee Swap is the name New England and much of the northeastern U.S. uses for the white elephant gift exchange. The mechanics match: wrapped gifts, numbered turns, and each turn you unwrap a new gift or take an opened one. The differences are cultural: the "swap" wording, and a gift pool that leans toward desirable items.

What is a Yankee Swap?

A Yankee Swap is the name New England and much of the northeastern United States uses for the white elephant gift exchange. Mechanically it's the same game: everyone brings a wrapped gift, players draw numbers, and on each turn you either unwrap a new gift or take an already-opened one from another player. The two real differences are cultural. First, the verb: Yankees "swap," Southerners "steal," and the politeness is entirely tactical. Second, the gift pool: Yankee Swaps traditionally lean toward genuinely desirable gifts — good olive oil, quality blankets, gift cards, nice booze — rather than a pile of pure gag gifts. The stealing is more intense when the loot is real.

Yankee Swap rules

  1. Set a real-gift budget

    $25–$50 is typical. Tell guests the pool skews useful and coveted — nobody wants to be the person who brought a whoopee cushion to a serious swap.

  2. Draw numbers

    Numbers from a hat set the order. In true New England fashion, someone will claim the hat drawing "always goes funny" for their cousin.

  3. Player 1 opens

    The first player unwraps a gift and shows it off. Their turn ends.

  4. Swap or open

    Each later player either unwraps a new gift or swaps with any opened gift. If your gift is swapped away, you immediately go again.

  5. Three swaps freeze a gift

    After a gift has been swapped three times, it's locked with its holder. (Many Boston-area families cut this to two for pace.)

  6. Player 1's revenge swap

    After the final number, Player 1 may swap with anyone — the standard fairness correction.

Where does the name come from?

The most repeated story ties the term to the American Civil War: Christmastime prisoner exchanges between Union ("Yankee") and Confederate forces. Historians haven't firmly documented that origin — it's folklore, and we say so honestly — but the term's home turf is unambiguous: New England and the Northeast, where it remains the dominant name to this day. Some purists maintain that a "Yankee Swap" is by definition the desirable-gift version of the game, with "white elephant" reserved for gag-gift piles. Most people use the names interchangeably — see the full glossary — but if your Boston in-laws correct you at the table, now you know why.

Gifts that thrive at a New England swap

Since Yankee Swap pools skew desirable, bring something people will actually fight over: a Yeti-style travel mug, a nice fleece or blanket, local maple syrup or a craft-coffee flight, a quality multi-tool, a good bottle of wine with an opener attached, or a gift card hidden inside a decoy gift. Yankee Swap FAQ

What is a Yankee Swap?

New England's name for the white elephant gift exchange — numbered turns, unwrap or swap, gifts freeze after 2–3 swaps, Player 1 gets the final swap.

Where does the name come from?

Popular lore ties it to Civil War–era prisoner exchanges at Christmastime; the etymology is folklore, but the term's home is firmly New England and the Northeast.

Is a Yankee Swap the same as white elephant?

Yes, mechanically. The common distinction is the gift pool: Yankee Swaps lean desirable, white elephants traditionally lean gag.

How is a Yankee Swap different from a Dirty Santa?

Region and attitude: Dirty Santa (the South) embraces the steal, often with looser caps. See the Dirty Santa guide.

Sources and notes
